I am writing to apply for the position of Monitoring and Evaluation Officer at CMMB Haiti. For
three years I was M&E Officer on an HIV program at Caris Foundation International, where I ran
the routine reporting of multi-site clinics and reported MER indicators to USAID through DATIM,
PEPFAR's DHIS2-based platform. Alongside that I work as a data engineer, administering the
databases and collection systems that this kind of reporting rests on. The posting asks for both
halves of that, and I can offer both. I live in Port-au-Prince, I work in French, English, and
Haitian Creole every day, and I am available to travel for site visits to health facilities.

The monitoring half is work I have already done on the schedule you describe. At Caris I
consolidated facility data on a fixed reporting calendar, and what made those figures defensible
was Data Quality Assessment: take an indicator, walk it back from the published report to the
source register, confirm it reconstitutes, then follow the corrective action until it is closed
rather than merely recorded. I established and supervised those protocols on MySQL-integrated
data across sites — the same discipline I would bring to your monthly, quarterly, semiannual and
annual cycles.

One lesson from that work shapes how I would handle facility reporting here. Early in my career
I produced a regional average that treated non-reporting sites as zeros. The number was
plausible, it triggered no alert, and it was badly wrong. Since then I have applied two rules
without exception: a missing value is never a zero, and no indicator is published without the
completeness rate it rests on. Writing that an indicator stands at seventy-six percent,
calculated on three hundred and fifty-three of three hundred and sixty expected reports, costs
five words and changes what a facility manager can actually do with the figure.

The HMIS half is where I expect to be genuinely useful rather than merely adequate. Supporting
IT infrastructure for data-management tools, pushing the current version out to every site,
reporting bugs, documenting the technical failures that leave a facility unable to reach its
system and its reporting in backlog — these are engineering problems, and they are the ones I
solve professionally. I have administered relational databases under concurrent access, resolved
the failures that make a system unusable in the field, and built the secure services and
role-based access controls around them. I know what the national system is: SISNU, on DHIS2,
across the ten departments and more than eight hundred facilities. Keeping a database deployed,
enhanced and functioning is not an aspiration for me; it is my other job.

Those halves meet in the part of the posting I find most interesting: building data culture at
the facilities themselves. I have trained field agents on ODK, KoboToolbox and CommCare,
administered the systems behind them, written the technical guides that outlast the training,
and automated quality checks in Python and SQL — because a control redone by hand every month
eventually stops being done at all. As an Applied Economist by training I can also carry an
analysis from sampling through to a trend, then state plainly to a facility team what it means,
what remains uncertain, and what decision it should prompt.

I am drawn to CMMB because it has been present in Haiti since 1912, because Haiti is its largest
country office, and because it works alongside the MSPP and the departmental health directorates
rather than around them. My own sector experience sits close to yours: Caris Foundation led the
consortium in which CMMB delivered Projet Santé in the North, and the HIV reporting I handled
there is the same reporting your PEPFAR-supported services generate. I would be glad to bring
that to a faith-based organization whose stated purpose is bringing healthcare closer to home.
Thank you for considering my application; I would welcome the opportunity to discuss it with you.
